# Interview preparation guide

Original fictional material for the Book Coach RAG demo. You may copy or adapt this guide for demonstrations.

## PDF page 1: Estimation questions: a four-step method

### 1. Clarify the scope

Restate what you are estimating, the unit, the time period, and the boundaries. Ask whether the interviewer wants a typical day or a peak day.

### 2. Break the estimate into drivers

Express the total as a few quantities you can reason about. For daily cafe drink sales, separate busy hours from quieter hours and estimate drinks sold per hour in each period.

### 3. State assumptions and calculate

Explain each assumption before multiplying or adding. Use round numbers so the interviewer can follow the arithmetic. Keep units visible.

### 4. Check plausibility

Compare the result with a capacity limit or a second rough estimate. Identify the assumption that could change the answer most and explain how you would validate it.

### A useful closing sentence

My estimate is a starting point, not a measured fact. I would validate the busiest-hour sales rate first because it has the greatest effect on this example.

## PDF page 2: Worked example: daily cafe drink sales

### Question and scope

How many drinks does one fictional cafe sell on a typical weekday? Count drinks, not customers. Assume the cafe is open for eight hours. These numbers are invented for demonstration.

### Drivers and assumptions

Assume two busy hours at 120 drinks per hour and six quieter hours at 40 drinks per hour. The daily total is the busy-period sales plus the quieter-period sales.

### Calculation

Busy period: 2 hours x 120 drinks/hour = 240 drinks. Quieter period: 6 hours x 40 drinks/hour = 240 drinks. Total: 240 + 240 = 480 drinks per day.

### Plausibility and sensitivity

Assume a capacity of 150 drinks per hour. The busy-hour estimate of 120 is below that capacity. If busy-hour sales range from 90 to 150, the daily estimate ranges from 420 to 540 drinks, holding quieter sales fixed.

### What to validate

Ask for point-of-sale counts by hour to check the assumed busy-hour sales rate. This example illustrates a reasoning process; it is not market research or a forecast.
